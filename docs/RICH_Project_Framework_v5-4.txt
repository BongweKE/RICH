                        DRAFT Concept Note
         AI4D Research and Innovation for Climate Hub (RICH)


Institutional Profile
CIFOR-ICRAF (the Center for International Forestry Research and World Agroforestry) is a
research centre with over 30 years of climate, land use, and agroforestry science across
more than 30 countries. In Africa, CIFOR-ICRAF operates offices and field programmes in
Kenya (Nairobi headquarters), Ethiopia, Cameroon, Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire, DRC, Tanzania,
Rwanda, Uganda, and Zambia.
CIFOR-ICRAF’s geospatial and land-use planning capacity spans more than a decade of
production-level implementation. Starting from the ALLREDDI project (Ekadinata et al.,
2010), the organisation built a full pipeline for spatially explicit, nationwide land-use and
land-cover change analysis across Indonesia: field data collection, satellite image
processing, classification, quality control, and integration into downstream policy analyses.
This capacity was formalised in the LUMENS platform (Land Use Planning for Multiple
Environmental Services), which has since been deployed across seven Indonesian
provinces as a negotiation support system for multi-stakeholder spatial planning (Dewi et
al., 2023).
CIFOR-ICRAF brings to the RICH Hub a distinctive combination: deep climate science and
policy knowledge at the forest-agriculture interface; continental-scale field presence in
Africa; and proven institutional capacity for the full geospatial production pipeline from
ground truth to policy-ready map.

Research Approach
Agroforestry as a Natural Climate Solution Blind Spot
In the cocoa-growing regions of Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, smallholder farmers have
cultivated shade-grown cacao for generations beneath native and secondary forest trees
(Van Noordwijk et al., 2025). These systems are productive, biodiverse, and carbon-storing
across multiple canopy strata. Yet every satellite map of West African forests, including
those used to enforce the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), classifies these gardens as
“forest.” Farmers growing cacao agroforestry face a legal fiction: their land is mapped as
forest, so any cacao harvested from it appears as a conversion from forest, making it non-
compliant under EUDR. The regulation was designed to stop deforestation. It is denying
market access to farmers whose land use prevents deforestation (Van Noordwijk et al.,
2025).
This story repeats across tropical Africa. In Ethiopia and Kenya, coffee agroforestry is
similarly misclassified (Dimobe et al., 2025). In the Sahel, parkland systems where farmers
deliberately maintain native trees on grain fields have no recognised class in any
continental land cover product (Boffa, 2000; Heiskanen et al., 2017). Homegardens across
East Africa, where multiple crops and trees coexist in small, intensively managed plots,
disappear entirely into the “cropland” category (Chirwa et al., 2024). The omission is
systematic. Every global and regional land cover map treats agroforestry as forest,
cropland, or an undifferentiated mosaic (Bourgoin et al., 2026; Lesiv et al., 2022). The maps
have no class for what agroforestry is.
Agroforestry, broadly defined as agricultural land where trees are deliberately integrated
into cropping or livestock systems, occupied approximately 43% of global agricultural area
in 2010, covering nearly 1 billion hectares where at least 10% tree cover was recorded
(Zomer et al., 2016). This category of land use is widespread across tropical Africa, where
surveys consistently find it present in 60–80% of smallholder farming households (Diouf et
al., 2025). The reason it is invisible on maps is traceable: no labelled training data for
agroforestry exists at tropical African scale. Recent initiatives such as Sample Earth
(Vantalon et al., 2025) have begun assembling machine-learning-ready reference datasets
for cocoa and coffee landscapes in Ghana and Vietnam, but coverage remains fragmentary
and does not extend to the full diversity of African agroforestry systems. We term this the
“dark matter of land cover maps.” Unlike dark matter in physics, the cause is tractable.
This invisibility has three cascading consequences for African climate action. First, EUDR
creates immediate trade barriers. In Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, which together supply 56%
of global cocoa, current EUDR reference maps classify shade-grown agroforestry as forest
with roughly 63% probability (Van Noordwijk et al., 2025). Second, carbon markets cannot
credit agroforestry’s climate contribution. Multistrata systems sequester carbon at 3.5–9.8
MgCO2 /ha/year (Teo et al., 2025), yet no map records this contribution because the class
does not exist. Smallholders cannot access climate finance for carbon that no map
recognises. Third, restoration planning lacks a baseline. Policymakers adopting
reforestation targets without knowing where agroforestry already exists risk displacing
working agricultural systems.
To our knowledge, no labelled training corpus grounded in community knowledge
currently exists at tropical African scale for distinguishing agroforestry from secondary
forest. Lesiv et al. (2022) produced the only global land cover product including
agroforestry as a discrete class: 49% producer’s accuracy, 5.8% user’s accuracy. Paluba et
al. (2026), using state-of-the-art AlphaEarth embeddings, achieves 91% overall accuracy
but explicitly excludes cocoa and coffee due to “lack of high quality open-access global
cocoa or coffee datasets.” African-specific studies confirm the pattern: remote sensing of
agroforestry in West African savanna and forest-agriculture mosaics consistently reports
the lowest per-class accuracy of any land cover type, driven by spectral overlap with closed
forest and structural variability across farming households (Piiroinen et al., 2017; Tamga et
al., 2022).
Rather than treating datasets or maps as stand-alone outputs, CIFOR-ICRAF positions them
as enabling infrastructure for a broader climate science and policy research agenda focused
on responsible AI deployment in African climate contexts. The project’s core contribution is
the generation of actionable knowledge on how AI systems shape climate adaptation,
mitigation, governance, equity, and climate finance outcomes across African landscapes
and policy systems.
CIFOR-ICRAF’s Research Programme for RICH
CIFOR-ICRAF will lead the synthesis of climate science evidence across the Hub, generating
policy-relevant knowledge on the opportunities, risks, governance implications, and scaling
pathways of AI-enabled climate systems in African contexts. CIFOR-ICRAF will also
contribute synthesis outputs, policy briefs, and comparative analyses to the Hub’s evolving
knowledge repository.
In this role, CIFOR-ICRAF proposes a focused research programme around three tracks,
described below. Agroforestry systems provide an analytically tractable and policy-
relevant entry point through which broader questions of climate-AI governance, scalability,
environmental sustainability, and equitable deployment can be investigated across African
contexts.
Track 1: AI-Augmented Climate Intelligence for Agroforestry Systems
We use the ALIA (Adaptive Landscape Intelligence in Africa) methodology to generate new
climate-relevant evidence on African agroforestry systems through AI-augmented
mapping, community validation, and spatial analysis. Initiatives such as Sample Earth
(Vantalon et al., 2025) and Helmets Labelling Crops (Nakalembe et al., 2025) have
demonstrated that open, geotagged reference data can improve the accuracy and
inclusiveness of land-cover maps for classes historically missed by publicly available
products. This track continues that effort with explicit focus on the diversity of tropical
African agroforestry systems, which remain absent from every continental and global land
cover product in use today.
Track 2: Climate Governance, Policy, and Climate Finance Pathways
We translate validated maps into policy-ready evidence: how agroforestry mapping enters
EUDR compliance chains, how AI-derived land use data feeds into REDD+ MRV and NDC
reporting, and how pilot map outputs connect to national spatial planning processes.
CIFOR-ICRAF’s research is embedded within the Hub consortium. Data and map outputs
are designed for use across the Hub network; AfriClimate AI’s seasonal forecasting and the
innovation cohort’s products benefit from improved land cover basemaps. Policy entry
points will be co-developed with LDRI and AfriClimate AI, drawing on LDRI’s relationships
with African policymakers and AfriClimate AI’s connections to farming communities.
This track also examines which AI-enabled climate applications are most scalable,
impactful, and responsive to the needs of vulnerable communities, directly supporting the
Hub’s broader research agenda on climate resilience and responsible AI deployment in
Africa.
Track 3: Scenario-Based AI Risks and Climate Futures in African Contexts
This track complements Tracks 1 and 2, ensuring that both technical development and
policy application are evaluated against climate, infrastructure, and equity outcomes.
Alongside the development of AI-augmented datasets and policy-relevant maps, CIFOR-
ICRAF will conduct scenario-based analysis to assess how different trajectories of AI
deployment may shape climate outcomes in African contexts. This track is directly linked to
Tracks 1 and 2, examining how the data pipelines, modelling approaches, and policy uses
developed in this project perform under different deployment pathways.
Two interrelated categories of risk are prioritized: environmental and infrastructure risks;
and representational and distributional risks.
First, building on the AI pipelines and data infrastructure developed under Track 1, and
their anticipated scaling through Track 2 policy applications, the project will model
scenarios of AI deployment across climate-relevant use cases such as land-use mapping,
MRV systems, and compliance monitoring. These scenarios assess how different choices—
such as model complexity, data volumes, frequency of updates, and compute location (local
vs. cloud)—affect energy demand, water use, and associated emissions. The analysis
identifies pathways through which scaling the project’s own approaches supports, or
undermines, climate mitigation and adaptation objectives in African contexts.
Second, we will examine how data gaps, classification choices, and validation processes
within Track 1 propagate through Track 2 policy applications, shaping who is recognised,
who benefits, and who is excluded. This includes modelling how misclassification or
absence of key land-use categories (such as agroforestry) affects outcomes in EUDR
compliance, climate finance access, and spatial planning decisions. The analysis identifies
conditions under which AI-enabled systems reinforce or reduce existing inequalities,
particularly for smallholder farmers, women, and indigenous knowledge holders.
Grounded in the project’s pilot landscapes but designed for generalisation across the Hub,
this track produces policy-relevant insights on how to steer AI deployment toward climate-
positive and socially equitable futures.



Objectives
  1.   New climate-relevant knowledge on African agroforestry systems is generated
       through AI-augmented, community-validated land-use intelligence.

  2.   Pilot AI-enabled land-use analyses demonstrate how agroforestry visibility can
       improve climate governance, compliance systems, and climate finance access.
  3.   Exemplary, relevant uses of the maps for climate-savvy spatial and/or green
       growth planning suitable to the African context are delivered.
  4.   Understanding, through models and scenarios, how different AI deployment
       pathways in African climate-relevant systems relate to risk and shape
       environmental outcomes, data representation, and distribution of benefits, to
       inform responsible and climate-aligned AI governance.
  5.   Synthesis research and policy engagement activities strengthen understanding of
       AI’s climate-related risks, benefits, governance needs, and scaling pathways across
       African contexts.
Research Outputs and Knowledge Products
Output 1: AI-Augmented Climate Knowledge Base for Agroforestry Systems
GPS-located land-cover reference samples for tropical African agroforestry and related
land cover classes, produced through AI-assisted visual interpretation and validated by
local practitioners. The dataset functions as a research and policy infrastructure layer
supporting downstream analysis of climate resilience, AI governance, compliance systems,
and equitable climate finance access. This contributes to the growing global effort to build
open, high-quality reference data that improves the accuracy and inclusiveness of land-
cover maps for classes currently missed by publicly available products. These mapping
gaps carry real policy and livelihood consequences: farmers whose agroforestry systems
are invisible on maps cannot access climate finance, face EUDR compliance barriers, and
are excluded from spatial planning processes that shape their land tenure.
The dataset targets approximately 2,000 validated reference points across 1 pilot
landscape chosen in collaboration with LDRI and AfriClimate AI; priority landscapes
include cocoa-shade systems in Ghana or Côte d’Ivoire and coffee-shade systems in
Ethiopia or Kenya. The classification scheme covers 8-10 main classes (multistrata
agroforestry, monoculture tree crops, natural forest, active cropland, improved fallow,
grassland, settlement, bare soil) with sub-classes for specific system types such as cocoa-
shade, coffee-shade, and Faidherbia parkland. Each point carries a class label, system sub-
type, validator identity and affiliation, GPS acquisition date, quality control score, and pre-
computed AlphaEarth embedding vector at the corresponding 10-metre pixel. Temporal
metadata links each point to its matching annual satellite composite, following the
approach established by Sample Earth (Vantalon et al., 2025) for cocoa and coffee in Ghana
and Vietnam. License: CC BY 4.0. Hosted on Dataverse.
Output 2: Policy-Relevant AI-Enabled Land Use Intelligence
Geospatial maps at 10-metre resolution for 1 pilot landscape in tropical Africa, showing
agroforest as a discrete land cover class. Each map includes per-class accuracy metrics and
uncertainty layers, produced through a classification pipeline trained on the AI-augmented
community-validated reference data and reviewed by local experts before release. License:
CC BY 4.0. Hosted on Dataverse.

Output 3: Multi-Stakeholder Capacities for Spatial Planning Integration Pathway
Output 3 delivers workshops and training on spatial planning integration pathways,
accompanied by policy briefs on utilising agroforest land use/land cover maps for spatial
planning, development, and green growth. These materials build the capacity of
government planners, NGO staff, and community representatives to interpret map outputs
and develop integration pathways into national spatial planning tools.
Output 4: Scenario-Based AI Risk and Climate Governance Analysis
Outputs 1 and 2 provide a basis upon which to build a set of scenarios that model how AI
system development and scaling across comparable climate use cases in Africa may impact
environmental sustainability, data representation, and equity outcomes. This output
contributes directly to the Hub’s research agenda on understanding the real impacts, risks,
benefits, and scaling implications of AI deployment in climate-relevant African systems.
This includes:
   -   Scenario-based modelling of AI deployment pathways (e.g., variations in model
       complexity, data intensity, update frequency, and compute infrastructure) and their
       implications for energy demand, water use, and associated emissions in African
       contexts.
   -   Analysis of how data gaps, classification choices, and validation approaches
       influence downstream outcomes in policy-relevant systems such as EUDR
       compliance, REDD+ MRV, and national spatial planning, with associated risks.
   -   Identification of risk pathways through which AI systems may reinforce or mitigate
       exclusion from climate finance, market access, and planning processes, particularly
       for smallholder farmers, women, and Indigenous knowledge holders.
   -   Policy briefs outlining governance and design strategies to align AI deployment with
       climate mitigation and adaptation goals, including recommendations on data
       practices, infrastructure choices, and validation frameworks.
Output 5: Climate-AI Governance and Policy Synthesis Products
This output generates comparative research, policy analysis, and synthesis products that
connect the project’s technical outputs to broader questions of climate adaptation,
mitigation, governance, equity, and responsible AI deployment in African contexts. Drawing
on evidence generated through Outputs 1–4, CIFOR-ICRAF will produce policy briefs,
comparative analyses, knowledge synthesis reports, and peer-reviewed publications as
appropriate, examining how AI-enabled climate applications influence climate resilience,
climate finance access, environmental sustainability, governance systems, and the
distribution of benefits and risks. These products contribute directly to the Hub’s objective
of generating actionable knowledge on the opportunities, challenges, governance needs,
and scaling pathways of AI for climate action in Africa.
These outputs are grounded in the project’s pilot landscapes but are designed to inform
broader Hub-wide research, policy engagement, and responsible AI deployment across
climate sectors in Africa.



Methods
Stage 1: Dataset Production
Dataset production follows three sequential sub-steps.
      Step 1a: Classification Scheme Development.
Experts and community members familiar with local land use and cover distribution
collaboratively define the classification scheme. Working sessions with agricultural
extension officers, government foresters, university researchers, and farmer
representatives establish the thematic classes, working definitions, and diagnostic
characteristics for each class. Community participants contribute contextual knowledge
that complements spectral signatures: seasonal phenology indicators, crop-tree
associations, land parcel size and shape, harvest evidence, pruning patterns, and
geographic associations between system types and landscape positions. This contextual
information directly informs the spatial priors applied in Stage 2 to constrain predictions to
agronomically plausible locations and reduce commission errors.
        Step 1b: AI-Assisted Data Labelling and Candidate Generation.
Experts and community champions with direct landscape familiarity (agricultural
extension officers, government foresters, university researchers) classify land-cover types
through AI-assisted visual interpretation of high-resolution satellite imagery (Google Earth,
Planet) and SPAM (International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI), 2024), which
provides disaggregated global crop distribution data to assess the plausibility of crop-type
presence at candidate locations. Agroforestry shares canopy spectra with neighbouring
forest cover, so spectral-only classifiers frequently confuse the two classes: per-class
accuracies for agroforestry consistently fall below those for forest in multispectral studies
(Numbisi et al., 2018; Uwizeyimana et al., 2023). Annotation therefore relies on contextual
cues that experienced interpreters can read directly from imagery: the irregular spatial
arrangement of mixed canopies against the geometric regularity of monocultures;
heterogeneous canopy texture from varied shade-tree heights; organic parcel shapes
following smallholder land boundaries; and spatial association with settlements or forest-
agriculture transition zones (Lelong et al., 2014; Numbisi et al., 2019). Each point is
labelled and quality-controlled; a random subset is independently reviewed by a second
interpreter for QA, following the approach of Sample Earth (Vantalon et al., 2025) and
Helmets Labelling Crops (Nakalembe et al., 2025). Community-verified reference points
then anchor a spectral-temporal similarity search using AlphaEarth embeddings (Brown et
al., 2025), 64-dimensional per-pixel vectors at 10-metre resolution integrating multi-
sensor observations (Sentinel-2, Sentinel-1 SAR, GEDI, ERA5), to identify candidate
agroforest pixels across the pilot landscape. Because spectral similarity alone does not
confirm agroforest, annotators visually validate all candidates against high-resolution
imagery; corrections feed an active learning cycle that progressively expands the reference
pool.
The pipeline also ingests CIFOR-ICRAF’s existing geotagged agroforest plot database, which
spans multiple years and geographic locations across the region. Public datasets such as the
Planted benchmark (Pazos-Outón et al., 2024) provide training data for monoculture tree
crops, settlements, and open agriculture, while intact forest reference datasets supply
conservative estimates for primary forest. Temporal alignment between plot observation
year and the satellite embedding layer is a technical requirement: each reference point is
matched to the corresponding annual AlphaEarth composite so that ground observations
correspond to the satellite conditions recorded in that year.
The classification scheme and data collection process are also designed to support
downstream scenario analysis by documenting sources of uncertainty, class ambiguity, and
data gaps, which serve as inputs into Stage 4 modelling of representation and distributional
risks.

Stage 2: Map Production
AI-augmented community-validated labels train lightweight classifiers on geospatial
foundation model embeddings. AlphaEarth Foundations (Brown et al., 2025) serves as the
primary backbone: 64-dimensional embeddings at 10-metre resolution integrating
Sentinel-2, Sentinel-1 SAR, GEDI lidar, ERA5 climate, and elevation data. AlphaEarth’s
Space-Time Precision encoder handles irregular tropical cloud cover by processing
variable-length time series. AlphaEarth has been validated on 25 Sub-Saharan African
countries for poverty mapping (Demaze et al., 2025) and on urban land cover in Greater
Accra, Ghana (Osei-Kuffuor & Soh, 2025), demonstrating that the embeddings encode
sufficient signal for African land cover tasks.
Contextual spatial priors (crop suitability, road networks) constrain predictions to
agronomically plausible locations. Contextual knowledge from Stage 1 classification
scheme sessions informs these priors, including community input on geographic
associations, typical land parcel sizes, and seasonal characteristics of each agroforestry
class.
Before maps are released, community members and local experts participate in
participatory map review sessions. Validators review draft outputs against their landscape
knowledge; corrections feed back into the accuracy assessment and, where warranted,
trigger additional label collection via active learning. Per-class accuracy metrics and
uncertainty layers are produced for the pilot landscape and reported in the open dataset
and associated publication.
Model configurations, data inputs, and processing requirements are systematically
documented to enable scenario-based assessment of how variations in model design and
deployment affect computational requirements and associated environmental impacts.
Stage 3: Capacity Building and Policy Integration Pathways
CIFOR-ICRAF’s LUMENS platform, deployed across seven Indonesian provinces for multi-
stakeholder spatial planning (Dewi et al., 2023), is adapted for African spatial planning
contexts. Training materials on land use/land cover map utilisation for spatial planning
integration pathways are developed and delivered. Policy briefs document how AI-derived
agroforest maps can serve as evidence for spatial planning, development planning, and
green growth commitments.
Government planners, NGO staff, and community representatives participate in
consultation workshops to develop integration pathways into national spatial planning
tools. Participants build capacity to interpret map outputs, understand accuracy metrics,
and apply evidence from the pilot map within their respective planning processes.
Stage 4: Scenario-Based Risk Analysis
This stage develops scenarios based on desk research and project data to assess how AI
systems used in land-use mapping and downstream policy applications may shape and be
shaped by environmental and equity outcomes in African contexts.
Scenario development builds directly on the technical and policy components of the
project. Using the data and classification systems from Stage 1 and Stage 2, and the policy
use cases identified in Stage 3, the project models alternative AI deployment and
development pathways. These include variations in model complexity, data volumes,
update frequency, and compute infrastructure (e.g., local vs. cloud-based systems).
For each scenario, the analysis assesses the environmental implications, including
projected energy demand, water use, and associated emissions from compute
infrastructure. It also reviews representation and distributional outcomes, including how
classification choices and data gaps affect visibility, eligibility, and access to climate finance
and compliance systems.
Scenarios are co-developed with consortium partners and stakeholders to reflect realistic
deployment trajectories within African contexts. Findings are synthesised into policy-
relevant insights that inform both technical design choices and governance frameworks for
responsible AI in climate applications.
Gender, Equity, and Inclusion
Gender and inclusion analysis is integrated throughout. Community validation prioritises
women farmers, youth, and indigenous knowledge holders. Field teams maintain
proportional women representation. Data governance ensures African partners retain co-
ownership of ground truth data. Gender-disaggregated analysis documents how mapping
benefits, including EUDR compliance support, carbon finance access, and recognition in
national planning, reach different farmer groups.
Stakeholder consultations also inform the development of realistic AI deployment
scenarios, capturing how map outputs are likely to be adopted, scaled, and integrated into
policy and planning systems, and identifying potential points of risk or exclusion.
Across all stages, the project prioritizes synthesis-oriented research that connects technical
outputs to broader questions of climate adaptation, mitigation, governance, equity, and
responsible AI deployment. Technical artifacts such as datasets and maps are treated as
enabling research infrastructure that supports comparative analysis, policy engagement,
and Hub-wide knowledge generation.

Known policy entry points are described below, noting that the project will remain flexible
and vigilant for further policy-related opportunities.
       2026: EUDR Compliance
The pilot agroforest map serves Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire, Cameroon, Ethiopia, and Kenya
national traceability systems. A map showing agroforestry as a discrete land-use class
transforms a compliance barrier into a compliance pathway. Over 71% of African countries
identify agroforestry in their NDCs but lack spatial baselines (Duguma et al., 2023).
       2025-2030: Climate Finance and MRV
Agroforestry maps fill data gaps in REDD+ monitoring systems, enabling results-based
payments. Ghana’s FCPF results-based programme has already disbursed $4.8 million for
verified agroforestry-related emission reductions, demonstrating that the financing
infrastructure exists; the data layer is what is missing.
       NDC Revision Cycles (2024-2026)
Most tropical African countries revise NDCs within this window. The pilot map provides
evidence for enhanced agroforestry targets.
       LUMENS as Policy Integration Pathway
CIFOR-ICRAF’s LUMENS platform (Dewi et al., 2023), deployed across seven Indonesian
provinces, provides a documented pathway for integrating AI-derived agroforestry maps
into national spatial planning. Its adaptation to African contexts is a core deliverable of
Stage 3.

EDI considerations
Early-career team members are selected through an open call prioritising women and
researchers from underrepresented African institutions. CIFOR-ICRAF’s Nairobi office
provides institutional support for African-based team members. Community validation
design ensures that farming communities, including women farmers, are participants and
co-owners rather than subjects of research.
